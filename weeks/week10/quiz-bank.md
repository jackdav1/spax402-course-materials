1. A team goes one-and-done in the conference tournament after the opponent shoots far above
   its usual three-point percentage, and the athletic director fires the coach. Which bias is
   that, and how is it different from hindsight bias?
   <!-- Outcome bias: judging the decision (or the coach) by a result that luck drove. Hindsight
        is believing afterward that the result was predictable. Probe the difference if they
        merge them. -->

2. Teams that go for two convert about half the time. Why might your own team's chance be lower
   than that, even with an average offense?
   <!-- Selection into the sample: strong offenses choose to go for it more often, so the
        observed rate describes the teams that chose to try (survivorship). Do not give the
        answer. -->

3. A manager praises his captain's "passion" for breaking a bat and calls the same outburst
   "unprofessional" from a bench player. What bias is that?
   <!-- Halo effect: one positive trait (veteran leader, captain) colors how the same behavior is
        read. If they say attribution bias, ask what makes the two players' cases differ. -->

4. A general manager keeps citing obscure stats to defend a struggling first-round pick. Name at
   least one bias they could be displaying.
   <!-- Anchoring (the draft-day evaluation is the first number and it won't move) and
        confirmation (searching for stats that agree). Either one passes; if they name one, ask
        whether another bias is also at work. -->

5. A coach yells at players after their worst games and praises them after their best. They
   usually play better after the yelling and worse after the praise. Does that show yelling
   works?
   <!-- No: regression to the mean. Extreme games are followed by more typical ones whatever
        the coach says. Ask what data would actually test yelling. -->
